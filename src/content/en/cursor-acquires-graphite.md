Cursor buying Graphite is a pretty important signal for anyone building AI developer tools. It isn't just another dev tool getting acquired. It's a reminder that programming is shifting from "how do I write one good line of code" to "how do I manage the huge volume of changes AI writes for me."
Over the past year, most of the competition has been about how strong the model is, how smooth tab completion feels, or how clever the refactoring is. But under competitive pressure these capabilities quickly become table stakes. The real moat is going to concentrate more and more in the workflow: who can embed AI-generated commits, PRs, reviews, rollbacks and audit logs deep into a team's existing processes and culture.
For teams that can't afford to burn billions of dollars training their own models, this is a pretty harsh reality. If your product is just one more wrapper around a model, sooner or later it gets eaten by the official IDE, a cloud platform or an open source tool. But if you focus on the decision points in everyday engineering, like who can merge, how to review code AI changed, how to roll back, or how to answer for security and compliance, then your position shifts from "tool" to "infrastructure."
I often flip it around and ask myself: if I swapped out every underlying model today and it made no difference which vendor's API I used, what unique value would the product have left? If the answer is "not much," that means it's a red ocean of model competition. The ones who survive long term will be those who make themselves the entry point to a team's workflow, not the ones whose AI is just a bit better than everyone else's.
